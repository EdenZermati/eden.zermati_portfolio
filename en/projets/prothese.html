Project question

How can three gestures be recognised from muscle signals and used to control a robotic finger?

Context

A myoelectric prosthesis uses the electrical activity of muscles to control movement. Electrodes placed on the skin record this activity. The resulting signal is called an electromyogram, or EMG.

In this project I worked on a prototype designed to recognise rock, paper and scissors gestures. The task was to connect signal acquisition, classification and control of a robotic finger.

My contribution to the team

I worked on this project with two other students. We started with hardware and some programs provided by our teachers. Our task was to adapt these elements and make them work together as a complete system.

Approach

I worked on the steps between the muscle signal and finger control. The system combines EMG processing, image classification and motor control.

See the five stages of the system

  1. Acquiring EMG signals

    We connected the MyoWare module to an Arduino. Measurements on the inner and outer sides of the forearm allowed us to compare muscle activity for each gesture.

    In our recordings, paper mainly activates the inner side. Scissors mainly activates the outer side. A closed fist activates both.

  2. Processing the signal

    The processing program applied a 1–500 Hz band-pass filter and rectified the signal. The next step was to extract its envelope.

    The envelope helps track muscle activity intensity over time and identify contractions more clearly.

  3. Creating scalograms

    I used scalograms generated with a wavelet transform in MATLAB. Each image shows how the signal’s frequencies change over time. It is then placed in the folder for the corresponding gesture.

    This representation turns gesture recognition into an image classification problem.

  4. Adapting a pretrained network

    I used transfer learning to adapt a pretrained network to the three gestures. This approach reuses features the network has already learnt from images. It provides a starting point for training on our scalogram dataset.

    The aim was to use an existing trained network with the project’s limited number of scalograms.

  5. Connecting recognition to movement

    The main program acquired the signal, created a scalogram and classified it. It then sent a command to a second Arduino controlling the finger’s servomotor.

    This allowed us to test the complete prototype from muscle contraction to movement.

Fabrication and assembly

The finger used 3D-printed parts based on the InMoov model. We filed the parts to fit and connected them to the servomotor with fishing line. Controlling the motor bent or extended the finger.

Results

12 / 12validation images correctly classified

The network correctly classified all four images for each gesture. This small validation set cannot establish reliability on new people or during continuous use.

During the final demonstration the prototype worked with some classification errors. Paper was the least reliable gesture. There was also a delay of a few seconds between contraction and finger movement.

See the validation results and training settings

Training took about two minutes with 20 epochs and 60 iterations. The network achieved 100% accuracy on the 12 validation images. This score applies only to that small image set.

What I learnt

With ten recordings per gesture, classification was still insufficient. Expanding the dataset improved the results. My main takeaway was that a good score on a few images does not show whether the model will work on other people.

The hardware also needed a lot of adjustments. We changed the electrodes three times and the board twice. We also fixed several communication problems between MATLAB and Arduino.

See the limitations and possible improvements

The image dataset remains small and all recordings come from the team. To evaluate the model more thoroughly I would collect more signals from several people. I would then separate training and validation by person and session.

The prototype controls only one finger. Further work could reduce latency, improve recognition of paper and extend control to several fingers with continuous recognition.

It would also be necessary to check whether the better score with more images reflects overfitting. The model needs to recognise new recordings.
