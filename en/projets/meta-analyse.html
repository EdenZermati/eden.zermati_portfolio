Project question

How can studies of phthalates and childhood asthma be combined to estimate an association while accounting for their differences?

Confidentiality

For confidentiality reasons I cannot share the results obtained during this internship. This page describes my role and the method used. The results and associated charts are not shown.

Context

This internship investigated the association between phthalate exposure and childhood asthma. My task was to assess whether findings from published studies could be pooled in a meta-analysis and then carry out the analysis.

A meta-analysis combines estimates from several studies to produce an overall estimate. Before running the calculations I needed to check that the studies were sufficiently comparable.

My contribution

I worked with another person on the preliminary steps. I then assessed feasibility and carried out the meta-analysis independently.

Data used

I worked with aggregated data reported in the articles. These were each study’s estimates and confidence intervals. The publications came from PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science.

The dataset included 23 studies using urinary measurements and seven using household dust measurements. I treated these two sample types separately.

Approach

The work began with publication selection and data preparation. I then carried out the calculations and checks in R.

See the six steps of my work

  1. Defining the question and protocol

    We structured the question using PICOTS. We then defined inclusion criteria and keywords. We used the Boolean operators AND, OR and NOT to build the search queries.

    The aim was to record our choices so the search could be repeated and the selection understood.

  2. Searching and screening publications

    We searched three bibliographic databases and removed duplicates with Zotero. We screened titles and abstracts before reading the selected articles. The selection was documented using PRISMA to make the process explicit.

  3. Checking what could be pooled

    I recorded study characteristics in Excel. I compared participant ages, sample sizes, study designs and sample types. I also checked for a measure of association with a 95% confidence interval. This could be an odds ratio, relative risk or hazard ratio.

    For this project I set a minimum of five studies per sample type and three per subgroup. These were project-specific criteria for assessing feasibility.

  4. Pooling estimates in R

    For adjusted odds ratios I used the logarithm and standard error. Adjustment accounts for other factors measured in each study. I examined heterogeneity using I² and Cochran’s Q test to assess variability between studies.

    In this project I used a fixed-effects model when I² was below 50% and a random-effects model otherwise. I calculated pooled estimates and confidence intervals with the metafor package.

  5. Checking sensitivity

    I used Egger’s test and funnel plots to look for signs of possible publication bias. I also removed studies one at a time to check whether a single publication strongly influenced the overall estimate.

  6. Examining subgroups

    I distinguished cohort studies from cross-sectional and case-control studies. I also considered exposure timing and the health outcome measured. Publications could concern prenatal or postnatal exposure and an asthma diagnosis or wheezing. I then combined two criteria to examine some comparisons in more detail.
